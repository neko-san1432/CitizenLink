ABSTRACT
This study presents D.R.I.M.S. (Data-Driven Reporting and Intelligence Management System), an intelligent complaint management framework designed to improve decision-making in local government units (LGUs) by transforming citizen complaints into easy to understand insights. Traditional systems rely on ticket-based processing, leading to data redundancy, inefficient resource allocation, and limited pattern recognition. To address these issues, the study integrates geospatial analytics and natural language processing into a unified system. The system utilizes an category-aware DBSCAN algorithm (with per-category epsilons derived via the Sorted K-Distance Method) to reduce over-merging and spatial fragmentation. A 3-phase Hybrid NLP pipeline (rule-based, TensorFlow.js fallback, and Human-in-the-Loop manual verification) filters non-literal or irrelevant reports, improving data quality before clustering. A fixed minPts = 2 threshold is applied to maintain clustering integrity while preserving sparse reports through an “ISOLATED” classification. The framework was evaluated using 288 geocoded reports from field simulations in Digos City. Results show an overall classification accuracy of 84.92%, with macro and weighted F1 scores of 0.83 and 0.85, respectively. The system also achieved a duplication detection F1 score of 0.97, a global Silhouette score of 0.82, and an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.96, indicating effective separation of meaningful hazard clusters from spatial noise. Performance profiling confirmed that the edge‑AI architecture is sufficiently fast and memory‑efficient for deployment on resource‑constrained LGU hardware. Overall, the study provides a scalable, data‑driven solution that enables LGUs to transition from reactive complaint handling to proactive, event‑based governance.
Keywords: Adaptive DBSCAN, Geospatial Analytics, Complaint Management System, Natural Language Processing, Spatial Clustering, Data-Driven Governance

CHAPTER I
INTRODUCTION
In an era of rapid urbanization and digital transformation, the ability of governments to respond effectively to citizen feedback is a cornerstone of democratic governance and public trust. Across the globe, from developing economies to large metropolitan centers, local governments are inundated with growing volumes of citizen complaints ranging from infrastructure defects to public safety hazards. However, many public sector agencies continue to rely on manual, ticket‑based complaint management systems that are slow, inefficient, and prone to error. Such systems are often described as “time‑consuming” and consistently fail to provide accurate or timely responses, leading to citizen dissatisfaction and a steady erosion of trust in public services.
For instance, an analysis of the municipal complaint system in Mumbai found that only 44% of roughly 20,000 complaints were actually addressed, with responsiveness to marginalised citizens notably lower [29]. In Indonesia, the national SP4N‑LAPOR! complaint platform has been shown to suffer from weak inter‑agency coordination, limited technical capabilities and low community involvement, preventing it from operating efficiently [32]. Similarly, research on the same LAPOR! system reveals persistent challenges in public participation, collaboration and transparency, which continue to undermine the country’s open government agenda [30]. In Ghana, a study using the DeLone and McLean IS Success Model found that while service quality, system quality, and information quality positively influence system usage and user satisfaction, considerable challenges remain in ensuring the optimal effectiveness of e‑government systems [31].
Consequently, the inability to process citizen feedback efficiently and to transform it into actionable geographic intelligence remains a universal challenge in urban governance, calling for a fundamental rethinking of existing complaint‑handling paradigms.
Background of the StudyEffective local governance relies on the ability to transform citizen feedback into precise, actionable intelligence. In the Philippines, this responsiveness is a critical metric for achieving the Seal of Good Local Governance (SGLG), which mandates that Local Government Units (LGUs) maintain high standards of transparency, accountability, and public service delivery [6]. However, despite these national mandates, Philippine LGUs frequently face severe structural challenges that obstruct responsive service delivery. For instance, a localized study of the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) in Nueva Ecija revealed critical shortages in the personnel and operational vehicles required for on-site verification (4). The results of this study explicitly demonstrate how these structural deficits directly degrade an agency's capacity to handle social complaints. When dispatchers lack the physical resources to manually verify an overwhelming influx of citizen reports, critical public service bottlenecks become inevitable.

The persistence of these bottlenecks is partly due to reliance on traditional, linear processing models. Such approaches treat each citizen report as a separate administrative task, leading to data duplication and wasted resources. For example, if ten different residents independently report the same fallen tree, a manual ticket-based system produces ten separate work orders that require individual tracking. To address these inefficiencies, researchers have proposed event-based spatial architectures that use geographic clustering to detect duplicate crowdsensed reports and merge them into single, actionable hazard events [15]. Compounding this issue, citizen complaints are often written in unstructured, colloquial, or code-switched language (e.g., Bisaya-English), making manual triage even more difficult.

In the specific context of Digos City, a rapidly urbanizing component city and the provincial capital of Davao del Sur, these operational limitations are increasingly pronounced. With a growing population of over 192,000 residents [28], the city is geographically susceptible to recurring localized hazards, particularly severe flooding and infrastructure degradation along the Digos River systems [27]. During these high-volume weather events, the City Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Office (CDRRMO) is routinely inundated with citizen alerts. However, instead of utilizing a centralized civic platform, these localized reports are highly fragmented, overwhelmingly submitted through informal channels such as official LGU Facebook pages and social media feeds [26]. Compounding this structural bottleneck, these crowdsourced alerts are communicated in a complex, trilingual vernacular blending Cebuano, Tagalog, and English ("Bislish"). Because standard automated systems are trained on pure English, they severely struggle to parse the unstructured, localized code-switching prevalent in the Philippine regions [25]. Consequently, because Digos City's administrative workforce operates with standard component-city resource constraints, manually reading, verifying, and geographically plotting hundreds of dialect-heavy social media comments onto a centralized map becomes an insurmountable task, leading to delayed emergency response.

However, transitioning to automated systems to solve these localized issues presents technological barriers. Standard spatial clustering algorithms such as K-Means assume rigid, spherical cluster shapes and cannot accurately map the irregular boundaries of real-world civic hazards [12]. Moreover, relying on cloud-based processing introduces severe server-side latency and bandwidth bottlenecks that are unacceptable during time-sensitive emergency responses [16]. Therefore, an urgent need exists for a localized, adaptive framework capable of intelligently filtering unstructured citizen complaints and geographically unifying multi-hazard reports without the limitations of rudimentary mapping or cloud dependency.

CHAPTER II
REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND SYSTEMS
Structured Deficits in Public Sector Complaint Management
Effective local governance requires the ability to transform citizen feedback into actionable, geographic intelligence. However, current literature indicates that public sector entities globally and locally are hindered by legacy data structures that fail to scale with modern digital reporting.
Existing Civic Reporting Platforms
Several civic reporting platforms have been deployed globally to bridge the gap between citizens and local authorities. FixMyStreet (FMS), launched in 2007 by the UK charity mySociety, allows citizens to report environmental defects (e.g., potholes, broken streetlights) directly to local governments via an online map [17]. SeeClickFix is a similar US-based platform that Desouza and Bhagwatwar (2014) classify as a citizen-centric and citizen-sourced data model where citizens generate reports but public agencies play a passive role with limited government-citizen interaction[18].
However, existing research reveals critical limitations. King and Brown (2007) found that despite FMS's ease of use, very little citizen discussion occurred such as reports were routed manually via email with no automated categorization [17]. More critically, Pak et al., (2017) analyzed over 30,000 FMS reports in Brussels and found significant sociodemographic inequality like low-income and ethnically diverse communities (North African, Sub-Saharan) were substantially underrepresented on the platform [19].

The Inefficiency of Ticket-Based Systems
The primary bottleneck in local government responsiveness is the reliance on linear, ticket-based complaint management systems. Research into public sector complaint resolution reveals that inefficiencies are largely systemic; for instance, Mogotloane and Louw (2024) demonstrate that poor complaint resolution in a developing country context is deeply rooted in severe capacity deficits and mismanaged communication infrastructures [8]. When applied to a localized context, this capacity deficit becomes a critical point of failure. A study analyzing the Department of Public Works and Highways (DPWH) in Nueva Ecija, Philippines, highlights that government dispatchers face massive limitations in personnel and operational vehicles required to physically verify incoming citizen reports [4].
The persistence of these public service bottlenecks is largely caused by the reliance on traditional, linear processing models. This approach creates a strict one-to-one correspondence between every citizen report and an administrative task, inevitably leading to massive data redundancy. For example, if ten different residents independently report the same fallen tree, a manual ticket-based system produces ten separate work orders that require individual tracking. This administrative duplication wastes severely limited LGU resources and creates unnecessary coordination burdens for staff. To address these inefficiencies, local governments must transition from reactive ticket clearing to event-based spatial architectures, utilizing geographic clustering to detect duplicate crowd-sensed reports and merge them into single, actionable hazard events [15].

The Volume and Noise of Social Media Feedback
Compounding the inefficiency of ticket-based models is the modern citizen's shift toward unstructured digital platforms. While providing accessible channels for civic reporting is vital, the integration of social media data introduces severe fragmentation. The Commonwealth Ombudsman (2014) explicitly warns that relying on decentralized social media channels (such as Facebook or Twitter) without a unified case management system creates massive challenges in information sharing between operational areas [10].
Furthermore, data scraped from these platforms is inherently noisy. As demonstrated by Baros (2023) in their spatial analysis of telecommunications complaints via Twitter, while social media provides real-time geospatial disturbance data, the sheer volume of unstructured text requires automated data crawling and mapping to yield valuable administrative intelligence [9]. Without an automated mechanism to semantically filter and geographically bind this unstructured noise, LGU dispatchers are left manually parsing through spam, metaphorical complaints, and duplicate alerts, thereby exacerbating the capacity deficits identified by Mantaring et al. (2019)[4].

Natural Language Processing (NLP) for Unstructured Civic Data
As established in the previous section, the primary barrier to efficient spatial mapping is the unstructured nature of citizen-generated text. Citizens rarely report hazards using standardized technical terminology; instead, they utilize conversational language, regional slang, and subjective phrasing. To bridge the gap between unstructured social complaints and actionable spatial data, modern intelligent systems rely heavily on Natural Language Processing (NLP).

Semantic Filtering and Spam Detection
The initial phase of processing crowdsourced civic data requires isolating actionable physical hazards from general grievances or spam. When citizens submit unstructured reports, manual dispatchers waste critical time reading non-actionable complaints (e.g., general frustrations about traffic) rather than deploying resources. Banga et al. (2023) highlight that implementing NLP architectures fundamentally resolves this by automating the comprehension process. By utilizing models trained specifically on complaint datasets, NLP pipelines can rapidly analyze text to identify underlying patterns, drastically outperforming traditional manual handling in both speed and accuracy [5]. In a public sector context, this semantic filtering acts as an automated triage layer, ensuring that only verified, actionable hazards are pushed to the geospatial mapping pipeline.

Automated Categorization and Routing of Urban Hazards
Beyond mere filtering, NLP is strictly required to categorize unstructured text into specific operational domains so that the correct local government department can respond. Bazzan et al. (2023) demonstrated this necessity by developing an Information Management Model that utilizes artificial intelligence to automatically read and extract technical defects from the unstructured complaints of local residents [11]. Their findings show that AI techniques can successfully translate everyday resident language into specific, actionable infrastructure categories.
This automated categorization directly reduces the administrative burden on dispatchers. Recent empirical studies, such as the automated complaint management framework developed by Mohamed et al. (2025), show that training machine learning classifiers on local complaint data successfully categorizes incoming issues into rigid operational sectors (e.g., academic, finance, or equipment) without human intervention [2]. By applying this classification architecture to civic hazards, an NLP model can instantaneously read a citizen's text and classify it as a Fire, Flood, or Road Hazard. Consequently, as Kormpho et al. (2018) assert in their study on smart complaint systems, integrating AI to automatically classify and route these demands prevents service delays, guarantees that reports reach the appropriate personnel immediately, and directly improves overall user satisfaction [3]. 

Spatial Clustering Algorithms in Geospatial Intelligence
While Natural Language Processing successfully filters and categorizes raw citizen feedback, plotting massive volumes of individual reports onto a geographic interface does not inherently optimize public service delivery. Without a mechanism to group overlapping coordinates, dispatchers are left visually overwhelmed by scattered data points. Therefore, modern geospatial intelligence relies heavily on spatial clustering algorithms to aggregate localized reports into continuous hazard zones.

The Necessity of Spatial Optimization in Emergency Response
Transforming individual data points into optimized spatial clusters is not merely a visual enhancement. It is a mathematical requirement for efficient resource deployment. In an analysis of emergency response infrastructures in mega-cities, Wang et al. (2021) demonstrated that utilizing multi-source geospatial data to optimize the spatial distribution of emergency services drastically reduces response times and improves urban safety [1]. By applying this principle to civic hazards, local governments can use spatial clustering to identify high-density disturbance areas such as a heavily flooded neighborhood and deploy bulk resources to that specific epicenter, rather than dispatching individual units to scattered, isolated tickets.

Limitations of Traditional Centroid-Based Clustering (K-Means)
To achieve this spatial optimization, a clustering algorithm must be applied to the map data. Historically, K-Means clustering has been the default algorithm for data grouping. However, literature shows that K-Means is fundamentally incompatible with unpredictable urban hazard mapping. K-Means is a centroid-based algorithm that requires the programmer to pre-define the number of clusters (k) before the algorithm runs, and it heavily assumes that data points will naturally form spherical shapes [20]. In a real-world emergency context, it is impossible for an LGU to know exactly how many hazard zones exist on any given day. Furthermore, real-world urban infrastructure damages such as a cracked water pipe running along a 5-kilometer curved highway do not form perfect circles, causing K-Means to inaccurately split continuous hazard zones.

Superiority of Density-Based Spatial Clustering (DBSCAN)
Because of the geometric limitations of K-Means, modern spatial mapping architectures require Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN). Unlike K-Means, DBSCAN does not require a pre-defined number of clusters. Instead, it groups points based on geographic density, meaning it only forms a cluster when a specific number of complaints occur within a strict spatial radius. Crucially, DBSCAN is capable of identifying clusters of completely arbitrary shapes, perfectly adapting to the irregular geographic realities of fallen trees, stretched fault lines, or flooded roads [12]. Furthermore, DBSCAN inherently features noise filtering; it isolates scattered, one-off outlier reports that do not meet the density threshold, preventing LGU dispatchers from wasting resources on unverified anomalies.
While DBSCAN offers advantages over centroid-based algorithms, prior studies have identified several limitations. First, DBSCAN is highly sensitive to its two input parameters: eps (neighborhood radius) and minPts (minimum points to form a dense region). Inappropriate parameter selection can lead to poor clustering quality, including merging of distinct clusters or mislabeling valid points as noise. Second, DBSCAN struggles with datasets containing clusters of varying densities, as it applies a global parameter set uniformly across the entire dataset. This limitation is documented in spatial clustering research, particularly for urban environments where different hazard types exhibit different spatial densities. Third, the algorithm becomes computationally expensive for very large datasets (O(n^2) time complexity), which may affect real-time performance on low-end hardware. The algorithm also cannot produce correct results when the database is large and densities vary substantially [21] [22]. Both works highlight the same fundamental limitations of DBSCAN. 
Client Side Machine Learning and System Architecture
While NLP and DBSCAN have been shown to address many theoretical challenges in civic data mapping, executing these complex algorithms introduces severe architectural challenges. Traditionally, government web systems rely on centralized cloud-computing architectures to process machine learning tasks. However, recent literature indicates that cloud-dependency is fundamentally flawed for time-sensitive, localized emergency management.
Latency and Bottlenecks of Centralized Cloud Computing
The primary limitation of server-side AI is its reliance on continuous, high-bandwidth internet connectivity. In an extensive architectural review of real-time Internet of Things (IoT) systems, Shi et al. demonstrated that centralized cloud platforms exhibit exceptionally poor scalability during crisis scenarios, inevitably causing server-side latency and bandwidth bottlenecks [16]. In an emergency context such as routing flood rescue units or clearing fallen power lines, delays caused by cloud server lag or connectivity breakdowns are unacceptable. Expecting underfunded Local Government Units (LGUs) to maintain expensive, high-availability cloud subscription models to process crowdsourced data is financially unsustainable and technologically fragile. To eliminate these bottlenecks, computational architectures must shift away from the remote cloud and toward decentralized edge computing.

Edge Computing and Web-Based AI (TensorFlow.js)
To bypass the financial and latency constraints of cloud servers, modern intelligent web systems utilize Client-Side Machine Learning. By deploying models via frameworks like TensorFlow.js, the system shifts the computational burden of text filtering and spatial clustering directly onto the client’s local device (i.e., the dispatcher’s web browser) [23]. This edge-computing paradigm means that the system does not need to send thousands of citizen complaints to a third-party server (such as an OpenAI API) to be categorized. Instead, the NLP inference and DBSCAN geographic rendering occur locally. This architecture guarantees ultra-low latency, as the interface can plot hazards in real-time without waiting for server responses, making it highly resilient for LGU command centers.

Inherent Data Privacy through Decentralized Processing
Beyond speed and cost-efficiency, client-side execution fundamentally alters the paradigm of civic data privacy. Traditional cloud APIs require raw, unencrypted citizen data to be transmitted across the internet to external corporate servers for analysis, introducing severe cybersecurity and privacy compliance risks. However, literature on Edge AI confirms that processing machine learning tasks at the network's edge inherently protects user data by keeping the raw information completely on-device [24]. By executing the NLP categorization and geographic mapping entirely within the local web browser, the proposed architecture achieves "Privacy-by-Design." No sensitive citizen locations or personal complaints are offloaded to third-party AI servers, fully securing LGU intelligence.

Synthesis and Research Gap
The increasing complexity and volume of public grievances require smart systems that overcome the slow manual screening and fragmented records of traditional complaint handling. Traditional complaint handling methods, such as call centers, email, and ticket systems, are described as “time‑consuming” and prone to inaccurate or inefficient responses, leading to customer dissatisfaction and lost customers [5]. In the Philippine local context, a study of the Department of Public Works and Highways in Nueva Ecija found that government dispatchers face severe limitations in personnel and operational vehicles to verify incoming citizen reports [4]. Evidence from a South African case study similarly shows that poor complaint resolution is associated with capacity deficits, inadequate communication, and a lack of coordination among responsible units [7]. To address these shortcomings, modern research validates the integration of Natural Language Processing (NLP) to semantically filter and classify unstructured citizen complaints [5]. Furthermore, spatial optimization methods, such as kernel density analysis combined with location‑allocation models, have been successfully used to identify high‑risk hazard hotspots and guide resource deployment in mega‑cities [1]. Together, these advances suggest that combining NLP with spatial analytics can help local governments move from reactive symptom treatment toward more strategic interventions.
Despite these technological advancements, a critical research gap remains in the architectural execution and data privacy of existing intelligent frameworks. The literature overwhelmingly treats NLP categorization and geographic clustering as cloud‑dependent processes. As demonstrated by Shi et al. (2016), reliance on centralized cloud computing introduces severe server‑side latency during emergencies and raises substantial security concerns when transmitting sensitive citizen location data. Moreover, existing studies have not examined client‑side, edge‑computing alternatives for civic hazard mapping. There is a distinct absence of decentralized spatial architectures tailored specifically for resource-constrained Local Government Units (LGUs) in the Philippines. No prior work has investigated whether a browser‑based, client‑side system that performs NLP filtering and spatial clustering locally without cloud dependence can achieve real‑time hazard mapping while preserving data privacy.

Statement of the Problem
Despite the availability of digital reporting channels, local government units (LGUs) in the Philippines still face three interlocking problems in handling social complaints. First, citizen reports are highly fragmented, submitted through informal channels such as social media, and written in unstructured, code‑switched language (Bisaya‑Tagalog‑English), making manual triage and categorization extremely difficult. Second, traditional ticket‑based processing treats each report as an isolated administrative task, leading to massive data redundancy when multiple citizens report the same incident. Third, standard cloud‑dependent systems introduce unacceptable latency and connectivity risks during emergencies, and existing spatial clustering algorithms (e.g., K‑Means) cannot handle the irregular, density‑varying patterns of real‑world hazards. These problems collectively prevent LGUs from transforming raw citizen feedback into timely, actionable geospatial intelligence. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following questions:
What is the system architecture of the innovative platform that manages social complaints?
What is the user’s perception of the developed platform?
Objectives of the Study
This study aimed to:
1. Design, implement, and evaluate the system architecture of an innovative platform that manages social complaints using a hybrid NLP pipeline and category‑aware DBSCAN clustering, measuring its classification accuracy, clustering quality (Silhouette, ARI), duplicate detection performance, and edge‑AI latency and memory footprint.
2. Assess user perception of the developed platform among non‑expert participants (simulating citizen and dispatcher roles) using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and the USE Questionnaire (Usefulness, Ease of Use, Ease of Learning, Satisfaction).
Significance of the Study
This study demonstrates a prototype platform that integrates a three‑phase hybrid natural language processing (NLP) pipeline (rule‑based, TensorFlow.js fallback, and Human‑in‑the‑Loop manual verification) and a category‑aware DBSCAN clustering algorithm to process unstructured citizen complaints entirely in a web browser. The significance of this study lies in its technical contributions and potential implications for local governance.
The study is significant for the following groups:
Local Government Units (LGUs). The prototype offers a low‑cost, client‑side alternative to cloud‑based complaint processing, requiring no continuous internet connection or subscription fees. By running entirely in a browser, it can operate on commodity LGU computers. The adaptive DBSCAN algorithm provides density‑based clustering of hazard reports without requiring pre‑defined cluster numbers, and the hybrid NLP pipeline filters and categorizes complaints with a rule‑based fallback to reduce reliance on large neural models. These features may help LGUs reduce duplicate reporting and prioritize high‑density hazard areas, though real‑world resource allocation benefits would require further field testing.
Citizens. While the prototype is designed for LGU dispatchers, a citizen‑facing submission interface can be added. The platform demonstrates how citizen reports could be systematically recorded and visualized on a map, potentially increasing transparency. Actual improvements in trust and accountability would depend on deployment and organizational adoption.
Public Safety. The prototype prioritizes urgent hazard types (e.g., fire, flood) by assigning them higher visual prominence on the map. This feature aims to help dispatchers identify critical reports quickly. The system does not replace emergency response protocols but serves as an auxiliary tool for situational awareness.
System Developers and Researchers. The source code and architecture provide a reference implementation for integrating TensorFlow.js with DBSCAN in a civic application. Developers can adapt the hybrid NLP fallback mechanism or the adaptive epsilon selection method for other domains.
Academic Community. This study contributes empirical data on the inference latency, memory footprint, and clustering quality of a client‑side NLP+DBSCAN pipeline for civic hazard reporting. It addresses the gap identified in the literature: the absence of a decentralized, browser‑based architecture tailored for resource-constrained LGUs.
Scope and Limitations
The scope of this study is the design, development, and evaluation of a client‑side prototype platform that integrates a three‑phase natural language processing (NLP) pipeline and an adaptive DBSCAN clustering algorithm for processing unstructured citizen complaints. The three NLP phases are:
Rule‑based phase. A lightweight dictionary of keywords and patterns (English, Filipino, Bisaya) that attempts to classify the complaint category (e.g., pothole, flood, fallen tree).
TensorFlow.js fallback phase. When the rule‑based phase cannot classify a complaint with sufficient confidence (e.g., low match or ambiguous input), a lightweight neural model running in the browser is invoked to classify the complaint.
Human‑in‑the‑Loop (HITL) phase. If the neural model also returns low confidence or the complaint is flagged as ambiguous, the complaint is queued for manual verification by an LGU administrator. The administrator can assign the correct category, which then updates the rule‑based dictionary or fine‑tunes the model incrementally.
The technical evaluation measures (a) end‑to‑end inference latency (ms), (b) peak memory footprint (MB), and (c) clustering quality using the silhouette score, comparing adaptive DBSCAN against standard DBSCAN. User perception is assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and the USE Questionnaire (Usefulness, Ease of Use, Ease of Learning, Satisfaction).
The prototype was tested using a dataset of 288 geocoded citizen reports collected by the researchers in Digos City. The reports cover five hazard categories: potholes, flooded roads, fallen trees, broken streetlights, and garbage accumulation. Field testing refers to the collection of location data and complaint texts in Digos City; the system was not deployed in live LGU operations.
User testing involved a convenience sample of 30 non‑expert participants recruited from the general population; none of the participants were employed as LGU dispatchers. Each participant performed a set of typical complaint‑handling tasks using the prototype in a controlled environment.
Limitations of the Study
The following limitations are acknowledged:
Small dataset size (288 reports). The dataset is limited in size and diversity, which may affect the generalizability of clustering and NLP performance results.
Simulated user testing. Participants were not actual LGU dispatchers. Therefore, SUS, USE scores may not fully reflect real‑world operational acceptance or the cognitive load of actual LGU staff.
TensorFlow.js model constraints. The browser‑based execution limits the complexity of neural models that can be deployed without significant latency. This is a known characteristic of client‑side machine learning frameworks.
Small linguistic dictionary for rule‑based phase. The rule‑based component uses a manually curated dictionary of approximately 400 words in English, Filipino, and Bisaya. This dictionary cannot cover all regional expressions or code‑switching patterns, causing the fallback TensorFlow.js model or HITL verification to be invoked frequently.
GPS accuracy. Geocoding relies on consumer‑grade smartphone GPS, which can have positional errors of several meters. This is a hardware limitation of commercial mobile devices.
Manual parameter tuning for DBSCAN. The epsilon parameter for DBSCAN was selected manually using a k‑distance graph. The adaptive variant is not fully automated, and performance may vary with different data distributions.
Single LGU context for data collection. Data collection was limited to Digos City. Results may not generalize to other LGUs with different geography, population density, or complaint patterns. 
Definition of Terms
Complaint Management System (CMS) – A web-based platform for receiving, monitoring, and resolving citizen complaints. In this study, D.R.I.M.S. serves as an AI-powered CMS designed to enhance complaint processing and decision-making.
DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) – A clustering algorithm that identifies dense regions of data points and labels low-density points as noise, used to detect complaint hotspots.
Adaptive DBSCAN – An enhanced version of DBSCAN that dynamically adjusts clustering parameters such as epsilon and minPts based on complaint categories.
Heatmap Visualization – A geospatial representation of complaint density using color gradients to highlight high-concentration areas.
Geospatial Analytics – The application of spatial analysis techniques to geographic data to identify patterns and support decision-making.
Geocoding – The process of converting textual location data into geographic coordinates for mapping and analysis.
Linguistic Truth Filters – A rule-based natural language processing approach used to detect and filter non-literal or misleading complaint text.
Noise (Spatial Noise) – Data points that do not belong to any cluster due to low density, often representing isolated or insignificant reports.
Geographic Information System (GIS) – A framework used for capturing, analyzing, and visualizing spatial data within the system.
Theoretical FrameworkThis framework integrates the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the DeLone and McLean Model of Information Systems Success (D&M IS Success Model) to guide the evaluation of the Digos Response and Intelligence Management System (D.R.I.M.S.). The integration of these models allows the study to assess both the behavioral acceptance of the system by users and the overall success and impact of the system. TAM explains how and why users decide to adopt the system, while the D&M model evaluates the quality, performance, and organizational benefits derived from its use[14][13]. Together, they ensure that the system is not only technically effective but also usable, acceptable, and beneficial in real-world governance.

Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) asserts that users’ intention to adopt a system is primarily influenced by two key constructs: Perceived Usefulness (PU) and Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU)[14]. These constructs determine the user’s behavioral intention, which ultimately leads to actual system use. In the context of D.R.I.M.S., local government personnel and system users must perceive that the platform improves their ability to manage complaints and make decisions efficiently (high PU), while also being easy to learn and operate (high PEOU).
These constructs are defined as follows in this study:
Perceived Usefulness (PU): The system is designed to significantly improve the efficiency and effectiveness of complaint management. This is operationalized through several core functionalities. First, the Adaptive DBSCAN clustering engine groups multiple related reports into a single administrative event, reducing redundancy and minimizing manual workload. Second, geospatial heatmap visualization enables decision-makers to quickly identify high-density problem areas, supporting faster and more accurate resource allocation. Third, the integration of natural language processing allows the system to filter irrelevant or misleading reports, ensuring that only high-quality data contributes to decision-making. These features collectively provide actionable insights, allowing local government units to transition from reactive complaint handling to proactive governance.
Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU): The system is designed to integrate seamlessly into existing workflows with minimal training requirements. This is supported by a user-friendly interface that presents complaint data through intuitive visualizations such as heatmaps and clustered event markers. Report submission is simplified through structured inputs and automated geocoding, reducing the effort required from users. Additionally, the system processes data in the background and presents results in a clear, interpretable format, eliminating the need for users to understand complex algorithms. This ensures that both technical and non-technical users can effectively interact with the system.

DeLone and McLean IS Success Model (D&M)The DeLone and McLean IS Success Model provides a multidimensional framework for evaluating system success through several interrelated constructs, including System Quality, Information Quality, Service Quality, Use, User Satisfaction, and Net Benefits[13]. In this study, the model is used to evaluate the performance and impact of D.R.I.M.S. within the operational environment of local government units.
The constructs are defined as follows:
System Quality: This construct refers to the technical performance of the system. This study includes system reliability, processing speed, and the efficiency of the Adaptive DBSCAN clustering mechanism. It also considers the responsiveness of the platform and its ability to handle real-time data inputs.
Information Quality: This refers to the quality of the system’s output. In D.R.I.M.S., it is measured by the actionability, relevance, and clarity of clustered complaint data, as well as the correctness of geospatial insights and filtered reports generated by the NLP pipeline.
Service Quality: This construct evaluates the level of support and usability provided by the system. It includes how well the system assists users in interpreting data, the clarity of its interface, and its ability to facilitate efficient complaint management.
User Satisfaction and Net Benefits: This construct reflects both the users’ overall satisfaction with the system and the benefits gained by the organization. It includes user experience in terms of usability and performance, as well as organizational improvements such as enhanced efficiency in complaint handling, better resource allocation, reduced data redundancy, and increased transparency and responsiveness in governance.

Conceptual Framework
This study is anchored in an integrated conceptual framework that combines principles from density-based spatial clustering, geospatial analysis, and natural language processing to guide the design, implementation, and evaluation of the Digos Response Intelligent Management System (D.R.I.M.S.). Specifically, the framework incorporates the Adaptive DBSCAN algorithm for dynamic spatial clustering, the Sorted K-Distance Method for parameter optimization, and linguistic filtering techniques for improving the semantic validity of citizen-generated reports. These components are further supported by system architecture design principles and user-centered evaluation through the System Usability Scale (SUS). Collectively, these foundations provide a comprehensive basis for understanding the system’s technical functionality, its effectiveness in enhancing data-driven decision-making for local government units, and its overall usability and acceptance among end users.

Core Algorithms
Hybrid NLP Pipeline
The Hybrid NLP Pipeline is the primary semantic gatekeeper of the system. It combines a high-speed rule-based engine with a TensorFlow.js AI fallback to distinguish credible citizen reports from linguistic noise. Its core functionality is to process trilingual input (English, Tagalog, and Bisaya) and apply "Linguistic Truth Filters" to dampen non-literal or speculative text. This ensures that only verified, literally intended complaints reach the mapping stage, reducing administrative "phantom data."
The workflow of the Hybrid NLP Pipeline in the system follows these steps:
Input Normalization: The system receives raw text and applies Levenshtein fuzzy matching against a 400-entry Bislish dictionary to correct typos and local dialects.
Pattern Matching: The rule-based engine scans for specific keywords and categorizes the report into the refined taxonomy.
Truth Filtering: The system checks for linguistic markers; if "walang" (negation) is detected, a 0.2 multiplier is applied; if "on fire" (metaphor) is detected non-literally, a 0.3 dampener is applied.
AI Fallback: If the rule-based engine cannot determine the category with high confidence, the sentence is passed to the TensorFlow.js edge-AI model for deep semantic analysis.
Categorized Output: The final result is a filtered, prioritized, and categorized text object ready for geospatial processing.

Adaptive DBSCAN Clustering Engine
Adaptive DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise) is the spatial mining algorithm used to group individual reports into unified "events." Unlike traditional mapping that uses a fixed radius, this engine uses the Sorted K-Distance Elbow Method to calculate unique search boundaries for different hazard types. Its primary advantage is its ability to ignore "noise" (isolated, unverified reports) and focus only on dense clusters.
The workflow of Adaptive DBSCAN in the system follows these steps:
Coordinate Projection: The system gathers all categorized reports and projects their GPS coordinates using the Turf.js engine and the Haversine Formula for spherical accuracy.
Epsilon Calculation: The system utilizes the Sorted K-Distance (Elbow) Method to plot the distance to the 4th nearest neighbor, identifying the optimal "epsilon" (search radius) for that specific category.
Density Search: The algorithm searches for groups of reports that meet the Uniform Spatial Integrity threshold (minPts = 2).
Cluster Formation: Points that meet the threshold are merged into an "event"which are points that do not meet the threshold are labeled as "noise" and hidden from the dashboard.
Event Validation: The system calculates a Silhouette Coefficient to assess cluster compactness and the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) to measure agreement with ground-truth incident groupings

Spatial Clustering Efficiency and Hazard Localization
Once the NLP engine verifies and categorizes a citizen report, the data pipeline streams the validated geographic coordinates into the spatial clustering engine. To group isolated citizen reports into actionable deployment zones, the system utilizes an Adaptive Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (DBSCAN) algorithm.
To ensure high data lineage and mathematical accuracy, the system applies strict temporal and categorical early-pruning prior to distance calculation. The algorithm isolates coordinates by category (e.g., Infrastructure vs Utilities) and enforces a localized 45-minute time window. Subsequently, it computes the Haversine distance between points using dynamically assigned MinPts and Epsilon(radius) parameters tailored to the specific hazard category.

Duplicate Detection Performance
The ability of the spatial clustering engine to correctly identify duplicate reports was evaluated using the ground-truth incident IDs (derived from _mission_title and _protocol_title fields). A report pair was considered a true duplicate if it shared the same incident ID, and a predicted duplicate if both reports fell into the same DBSCAN cluster (excluding noise). The evaluation was performed exactly over all 41,328 possible report pairs.
The system achieved a precision of 94.6%, a recall of 99.5%, and an F1-score of 97.0%. The high recall indicates that the DBSCAN clustering merges almost all true duplicate reports, while the precision confirms that false merges (different incidents being clustered together) are rare. These results demonstrate that the category-aware DBSCAN with eps = 200 m and minPts = 2 is highly effective at grouping multiple citizen reports of the same physical hazard into a single actionable event.

Human-in-the-Loop Review Queue 
The Human-in-the-Loop mechanism operates within the dashboard's "Train the System" module. It is a continuous learning process where local government officials review reports that the NLP engine could not classify with high confidence. By combining automated algorithms with human judgment, the system ensures accurate data categorization while progressively improving its own predictive accuracy over time.
Uncertainty Routing - When the NLP engine processes a citizen report and yields a low confidence score or detects edge cases (like figurative language or complex dialects), the system automatically flags the report and routes it to a pending manual review queue.
Manual Verification - LGU administrators review these flagged cases. They analyze the citizen's original description and either validate the AI's initial prediction or manually correct the report to the proper category and priority tier.
Active Learning -The system captures these human corrections and feeds them back into the machine learning model as new training data. This continuous feedback loop teaches the NLP engine to recognize similar patterns in the future, thereby increasing its overall accuracy and reducing the need for future manual intervention.

Discussion
System Architecture of the Innovative Platform (RQ1)
The results reveal that the DRIMS platform successfully addresses structural deficits in public‑sector complaint management through a three‑layer decentralized architecture consisting of the Client Layer, Backend and Logic Layer, and Data and Persistence Layer. The system utilizes a two‑stage intelligence engine that first categorizes unstructured reports via a three-phase Hybrid NLP Pipeline and subsequently groups them using an Adaptive DBSCAN engine. This architectural shift enables the municipality to treat citizen feedback as unified geographic events rather than isolated, redundant administrative tasks.

The system's core logic is distinguished by its use of Edge‑AI, shifting the computational burden of machine learning tasks directly onto the client's local device. This approach effectively eliminates server‑side latency and bandwidth bottlenecks often associated with cloud‑dependent systems. Furthermore, the physical architecture supports practical interaction through a three‑step reporting wizard for citizens and an Intelligence Dashboard for LGU officials, which features interactive heatmaps and real‑time WebSocket updates. These components enable administrators to visualize problem hotspots and prioritize resource allocation without the need for high‑availability cloud subscriptions.

The technical integrity of the system further supports its architectural effectiveness. The validation results confirmed a Silhouette score of 0.82 and an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.96, indicating that the system effectively separates meaningful hazard clusters from spatial noise.

User Perception of the Developed Platform (RQ2)
The evaluation of user perception indicates that the platform is both functionally effective and highly usable within the context of local governance. Participants, consisting of a convenience sample of 30 individuals, assessed the system using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and USE Questionnaire. These positive evaluations are anchored in the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), where high scores in Perceived Usefulness (PU) were driven by the system’s ability to reduce data redundancy through adaptive clustering. Users perceived the Perceived Ease of Use (PEOU) to be high due to the intuitive presentation of complaint data through intensity-gradient heatmaps and clustered event markers.

By integrating a Human‑in‑the‑Loop (HITL) mechanism, the platform allows officials to manually verify ambiguous reports, ensuring that the AI progressively improves while administrators retain final judgment over edge cases. Overall, the combination of an accurate backend and a user‑friendly interface led users to perceive the platform as a highly effective tool for transitioning from reactive to proactive governance.


CHAPTER V
CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
Conclusion
This study presents D.R.I.M.S. (Data‑Driven Reporting and Intelligence Management System), an intelligent complaint management framework designed to improve decision making in local government units (LGUs) by transforming citizen complaints into easy‑to‑understand insights. Traditional systems rely on ticket‑based processing, leading to data redundancy, inefficient resource allocation, and limited pattern recognition. To address these issues, the study integrates geospatial analytics and natural language processing into a unified system. The system utilizes a category‑aware DBSCAN algorithm (with per‑category epsilons derived via the Sorted K‑Distance Method) to reduce over‑merging and spatial fragmentation. A three‑phase Hybrid NLP pipeline (rule‑based, TensorFlow.js fallback, and Human‑in‑the‑Loop manual verification) filters non‑literal or irrelevant reports, improving data quality before clustering. A fixed minPts = 2 threshold is applied to maintain clustering integrity while preserving sparse reports through an “ISOLATED” classification. The framework was evaluated using 288 geocoded reports from field simulations in Digos City. Results show an overall classification accuracy of 84.92%, with macro and weighted F1‑scores of 0.83 and 0.85, respectively. The system also achieved a duplicate detection F1‑score of 0.97, a global Silhouette score of 0.82, and an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of 0.96, indicating effective separation of meaningful hazard clusters from spatial noise. Performance profiling confirmed that the edge‑AI architecture is sufficiently fast and memory‑efficient for deployment on resource‑constrained LGU hardware. Overall, the study provides a scalable, data‑driven solution that enables LGUs to transition from reactive complaint handling to proactive, event‑based governance.

Recommendations 
The following recommendations are provided to ensure the continued effectiveness of the platform:
For Local Government Units (Digos City):
Formally adopt the event‑based spatial architecture to eliminate data redundancy and optimize the triage of limited municipal resources.
Regularly update the trilingual dictionary through the Human‑in‑the‑Loop feedback mechanism to capture new local slang and evolving civic terminology.
Use the dashboard’s heatmap and cluster views to identify high‑density hazard zones and proactively deploy resources, rather than reacting to individual tickets.
For Citizens:
Utilize the trilingual reporting features (English, Tagalog, Bisaya) to ensure that localized issues are captured with high descriptive clarity in the preferred language.
For Future Researchers:
Conduct extended field testing across neighboring municipalities to validate the adaptability of the DBSCAN search parameters (eps, minPts) in different urban densities and cultural‑linguistic contexts.
Explore the integration of predictive analytics (e.g., time‑series forecasting of complaint volumes) to anticipate service failures before they occur.
Benchmark the current category‑aware DBSCAN against alternative algorithms such as HDBSCAN to compare cluster stability and parameter sensitivity.

System Enhancements 
Enhance: Expand the NLP dictionary by implementing an active learning loop where HITL corrections automatically update the rule‑based keyword set and retrain the TensorFlow.js fallback model.
Optimize: Refine the backend spatial processing engine to handle high‑volume concurrent data streams (e.g., during city‑wide emergencies) while maintaining real‑time performance.
Integrate: Add alternative submission channels such as SMS or Viber gateways to allow citizens without consistent internet access to submit geocoded reports.

Future Research Directions 
Validate the system’s generalizability by deploying a pilot within an actual LGU operations center, measuring real‑world response time improvements and user acceptance among professional dispatchers.
Extend system capabilities by training machine learning models to predict recurrent infrastructure failures based on historical spatial heatmaps and temporal patterns.
Investigate the use of federated learning to improve NLP classification across multiple LGUs without sharing raw citizen data.model.

Summary of Findings
The study successfully met its objectives by developing a functional spatial intelligence platform and evaluating its technical performance using 288 geocoded reports from Digos City (89 HITL‑excluded, 199 automatically classified).
Classification accuracy: 84.92% overall, with macro F1 = 0.83 and weighted F1 = 0.85. Sanitation reports showed the highest semantic overlap with Environment (9 misclassifications).
Clustering quality: Category‑aware DBSCAN (eps = 200 m, minPts = 2) produced 8 clusters with zero noise, a Silhouette score of 0.82, and an ARI of 0.96.
Duplicate detection: F1 score of 0.97 (precision 94.6%, recall 99.5%).
Edge‑AI performance: Rule‑based engine averaged 0.72 ms (85% of reports); TensorFlow.js fallback averaged 265 ms (15% of reports). Peak memory usage was 66.3 MB.
These findings confirm that a client‑side, event‑based architecture can effectively transform unstructured citizen complaints into actionable geospatial intelligence without cloud dependency.
